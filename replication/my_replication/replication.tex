	\section*{1. Original Study}
	
	The study I have chosen to replicate is "Kreiman, G., \& Masullo, J. (2020). Who Shot the Bullets? Exposure to Violence and Attitudes Toward Peace: Evidence from the 2016 Colombian Referendum. Latin American Politics and Society, 62(4), 24–49. doi:10.1017/lap.2020.14". 
	
	This study explores a potential reasoning behind the surprising results of the 2016 Peace Referendum in Colombia. The referendum was brought to a public vote in 2016 by the Colombian president Juan Manuel Santos, following peace talks and a subsequent ceasefire agreeement between the Colombian government and the Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (referred to as "FARC" from this point forward). FARC were known as the largest and most powerful guerilla group operating throughout the 50 years of the Colombian civil war. The referendum also included provisions for rural reform, strategies to reduce coca growth and drug trafficking, as well as the allowance of FARC participating in elections. The public vote resulted in the referendum not being passed by an incredibly small margin. Santos and FARC subsequently continued negotiations and Santos passed the referendum through Colombian congress in late 2016.
	
	Due to surprising results of the public referendum vote, researchers began to explore why this might have been the case. Preliminary research determined that there were clear geographical patterns within the votes, which was also a characteristic of patterns of violence throughout the war. Subsequent hypotheses have stated that support for the referendum was higher in areas more strongly impacted by war-time violence, as well as that areas under FARC control voted against the referendum due to the fact that it did not appear to punish FARC enough. Ultimately, there have been a great amount conflicting beliefs as to why the referendum did not go through on the public vote. Kreiman and Masullo discovered a gap in the research regarding how variation in people's experiences of violence might have impacted their attitudes towards peace, and ultimately the way they voted in the referendum. They hypothesized that the identity of the perpetrator matters in how people are affected by violence and their subsequent attitude towards peace. Specifically, that individuals in municipalities more impacted by FARC violence were more supportive of the referendum, whereas people more impacted by paramilitary violence were less supportive. The main paramilitary group considered in this investigation was BACRIM (criminal bands in spanish), a paramilitary group formed following the demobilization of the United Self Defence Forces of Colombia (AUC). The AUC was a paramilitary group formed in the 1980s to combat the advancements of FARC.
	
	\vspace{.5cm}
	
	\textbf{Hypotheses:}
		\begin{itemize}
			\item In areas where Group A has committed most of the violence, residents are more likely to support a peace agreement with Group A.
			\item In areas where B has committed most of the violence, residents are less likely to support a peace agreement with Group A.
		\end{itemize}
	
	\newpage
	\textbf{Method:}
	
	In order to determine how violence perpetrated by FARC and/or paramilitaries/BACRIM impacted the victims' votes in the 2016 referendum, Kreiman and Masullo gathered previously collected data on overall exposure to violence in municipalities throughout Colombia, number of attacks perpetrated by each group in each municipality, the percentage of votes per municpality supporting the referendum, as well as various control variables. This was a quantitative study utilizing an ordinary least squares regression with departmental fixed effects to make inferences regarding their research question and hypotheses.

	\section*{4. My Contribution}
	
	My contribution was spurred by the discovery of non-identical intercept values following replication of the regression code. This led to the realization that the authors did not use a consistent reference category for their department fixed effects dummy variable. After discovering this, I realized that some of the department groups contain very few municipalities within them and therefore have an incredibly small group size. These small, unbalanced group sizes do not allow for estimation of within-group variations and resultingly do not produce precise or accurate estimates. Controlling for fixed effects is still important, as there is variation between departments that may impact the results and need to be controlled for.
	
	In order to do this accurately, I implemented a multilevel model (MLM) to estimate the regression coefficients. MLM is a method that is an extension of linear regression, but allows for more accurate inferences for grouped data by allowing the intercept, or both the intercept and slopes, to vary. Group and individual level variation is considered within the estimation of the group-level coefficients, allowing for accurate inferences to be made for groups that have small sample sizes, which is highly appropriate in this setting. An important concept in MLM that helps explain how it functions is pooling, which refers to the way in which information is shared across groups in a model. A "no pooling" model is one in which no information is shared between groups and the coefficients are estimated purely based on variation within their group. A full pooling model means that groups are not considered, ignoring differences that might arise from between group variation. An MLM is in between these two pooling extremes, allowing group-level estimates to be a weighted combination of within and between group data. A small group size will then pull more data from the overall larger sample average, rather than from the information within its group, which is necessary for this data (Gelman \& Hill, 2017). 
		
	For this particular instance, there will be two levels, the department level and the municipality level. A varying intercept model is utilized, allowing the intercept to vary for each group, but keeping the slopes the same.

	\section*{5. Another Contribution/Correction}
	
	Upon initially attempting to interpret the coefficients from the classic OLS model, I came across what I believe is a fault in the author's interpretations. Verbatim, the authors interpret the (ln) FARC attacks and (ln) Para/BACRIM attacks coefficients as: "If the mean of FARC attacks in a given municipality increases by 1\%, the yes vote in the referendum increases by approximately 1.06 points." and "If the mean of paramilitary/BACRIM attacks in a municipality increase by 1\%, the yes vote in the referendum decreases by approximately 1.32 points."
	
	This interpretation is not entirely accurate, as the log-transformation of the raw FARC and Para/BACRIM attacks counts, and subsequently the interpretation of a 1\% increase in the mean of these attacks would not allow the authors to then directly interpret the coefficients without transforming them as well. Taking the log of the attack counts requires the $Beta$ coefficient value to then be divided by 100, to account for the transformation of x. This would then result in the interpretations being what I reported when presenting the OLS output results. The difference in value when considering and applying this transformation is incredibly large, greatly changing the magnitude of the results.
	
	Following this realization, I chose to determine predicted probabilities of \% yes votes in referendum for varying numbers of FARC attacks and para/BACRIM attacks, and subsequently plot these values against the exponentiated (ln) FARC Attacks and (ln) Para/BACRIM Attacks variable values to get the true raw counts.
	
	To do this, I created a new dataframe by repeating the first row of the original dataframe 100 times to create new data to make my prediction from. I added a column for the departments and held this at 0. For the covariates, I held them all at their mean apart from \texttt{ln\_atfarc\_cede} and \texttt{ln\_atfarc\_cede}, for which I used a variation of numbers between the min and max values. Following this, I created my predictions and calculated confidence intervals before plotting the results against the true FARC and para/BACRIM attack counts.

The lines illustrate a logarithmic curve in the relationship between the FARC attack counts and the \% yes votes in the referendum, as well as the para/BACRIM attack counts and the \% yes votes in the referendum. This indicates that the $Beta$ coefficient for the impact of the log transformation of these attacks needs to be divided by 100. The true interpretation for the \texttt{(ln) FARC attacks} and \texttt{(ln) Para/BACRIM} coefficients are:
	
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	
	\textbf{FARC Attacks:} Holding all other variables constant, for every 1\% increase in the mean log of FARC attacks in a given municipality, the yes vote in the referendum increases by approximately 0.0106 points.
	
	\textbf{Para/BACRIM Attacks:} Holding all other variables constant, for every 1\% increase in the mean log of para/BACRIM attacks in a given municipality, the yes vote in the referendum decreases by approximately 0.0132 points.
	
	\vspace{0.2cm}
	
	Within these figures, I also plotted the 95\% confidence intervals, which are incredibly large, but do not vary among varying numbers of FARC or para/BACRIM attacks due to setting all other covariates at their mean to generate the predicted \% yes vote in the referendum. These confidence intervals do illustrate the fact that municipalities with very high and very low numbers of FARC or para/BACRIM attacks could ultimately have the same \% yes votes in the referendum, due to the fact that the confidence intervals at both high and low counts of FARC and para/BACRIM attacks include the same values. I chose to add a rug plot along the x-axis to illustrate the distribution of the true number of FARC and para/BACRIM attacks, which can be seen as incredibly skewed to the right, indicating that in most municipalities, and subsequently departments, there were very few numbers of FARC or para/BACRIM attacks. 
	
	With all of this being considered, the authors might have chosen to log the counts of FARC and para/BACRIM attacks to create a more uniform distribution and subsequently make their results interpretable using an OLS regression. It is regretable that this subsequently makes their interpretation incorrect, and resultingly significantly decreases the magnitude of their findings.
